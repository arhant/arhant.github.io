Explainer · The black hole at the bottom of this site

What you'd see falling into a black hole

The Contact section of this site ends at a black hole you can fall into. Here is what the renderer shows, and how much of it real physics agrees with.

Take the plunge

Opens the Contact section. In the 3D version, choose “Cross the event horizon” there: about 25 seconds of animation, and Escape brings you back.

The black hole on this site

It is a Schwarzschild black hole: one that does not spin, the simplest kind general relativity allows. Sizes on this page are given in units of its Schwarzschild radius, Rs, the radius of its event horizon.

The renderer traces the paths light takes through the curved space around the hole, backwards from the camera. The Milky Way behind the hole and the hole's own accretion disk are bent the way gravity would bend them, so the effects below are not painted on: they come out of the light paths.

The shadow is bigger than the hole

The dark disc in the middle is not the event horizon. Any ray of light aimed to pass the centre closer than about 2.6 Rs (272 Rs, to be exact) is pulled in, so from far away the hole casts a shadow about 2.6 times the size of its horizon.

The Event Horizon Telescope's 2019 image of M87*, a black hole about 6.5 billion times the mass of the Sun, shows a dark centre inside a ring of light, consistent with what general relativity predicts.

The photon ring

At 1.5 Rs is the photon sphere, where gravity bends light into a circle. Light that skims it can loop part of the way, or more than once, around the hole before escaping, and those rays pile up into a thin, bright ring that hugs the edge of the shadow.

Lensing wraps the disk over the top

The accretion disk is a flat ring of hot gas orbiting in the hole's equatorial plane. Light from the part of the disk directly behind the hole is bent over the top of the shadow and under the bottom, so you see the far side of the disk as arcs above and below the hole, even though it is hidden behind it. Seen nearly edge-on, as on this site, the disk seems to wrap around the hole.

One side is brighter

At its inner edge the gas orbits at about half the speed of light. Light from the side moving toward you is shifted to higher frequencies and beamed forward, so that side looks brighter and bluer; the receding side looks dimmer and redder. This is Doppler beaming.

For Interstellar, the filmmakers chose to leave the Doppler shifts out so the image would read clearly for a general audience; James, von Tunzelmann, Franklin and Thorne (2015) explain that choice. This site keeps the effect, at partial strength.

Falling in

Whether you would survive the trip depends on the hole's size. Tides, the difference between gravity's pull on your head and on your feet, are fierce near a black hole a few times the mass of the Sun: they would pull you apart before you reached the horizon. Near a supermassive black hole they are gentle at the horizon, and you would cross it without feeling anything special. They grow as you fall further in; Hamilton estimates that, for a hole big enough to let you in intact, they become fatal only about a tenth of a second before the singularity.

The site's plunge follows a “raindrop”: an observer who falls straight in, starting from rest far away. At distance r, the raindrop moves at β=Rs/r times the speed of light relative to an observer hovering at that distance, reaching the speed of light at the horizon. Two effects of that motion change the view:

  • Aberration. Just as rain seems to come at you from ahead when you run, your motion shifts the directions light seems to arrive from toward the way you are heading.
  • Doppler shift. Light from directly overhead, from the universe you are leaving, arrives with its frequency multiplied by 11+β. At the horizon, where β reaches 1, it arrives at half its original frequency: redder and dimmer. Light from other directions shifts by other amounts, and the renderer applies the matching shift in every direction.

Nothing marks the horizon

There is no surface at the event horizon and nothing to see as you cross it. Locally it is just more empty space: in Hamilton's simulated journey you cannot tell when you pass through it, and the view of the outside universe does not disappear. What changes is where you can go. Past the horizon every path leads inward, whatever you do, and no signal you send can get back out.

The singularity is a moment, not a place

Inside the horizon, the direction toward the centre behaves like the direction of time. The singularity is not a point in front of you that you could steer around; it lies in your future, as surely as tomorrow does.

For a non-spinning hole as massive as M87*, a raindrop would take hours of its own time to get from the horizon to the singularity. The site compresses the whole fall into about 25 seconds and ends it there.

Where the site simplifies

  • No spin. Real black holes are expected to spin. Spin makes the shadow slightly lopsided and moves the disk's inner edge; the site uses the simpler non-spinning case.
  • Artistic exposure. Brightness is tuned for a screen, and during the plunge an automatic exposure adapts the way an eye or a camera would. The colours are not a measurement.
  • Partial beaming. The disk's Doppler brightening is applied at partial strength, so its dim side stays readable.
  • A stylised disk. The disk follows a standard thin-disk temperature profile, but its texture is procedural noise, not a simulation of real gas.
  • Paced, not timed. The plunge lasts about 25 seconds for watching; it is not timed like a real fall.
  • The sky. The background is a baked, Milky Way-like sky, not a map of real stars.
